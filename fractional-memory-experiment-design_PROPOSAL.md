## Optimal Experimental Design for Identifying Ecological Memory

### Working title

**How Should an Ecosystem Be Perturbed to Identify Memory? Optimal Experimental Design for Fractional, Delayed, and Latent-State Ecological Models**

### Alternative titles

- **Optimal Perturbations for Discriminating Fractional Memory in Predator–Prey Systems**

## 1. Executive summary

Fractional ecological models are often justified by claiming that biological systems exhibit memory. However, observational population time series frequently cannot distinguish a fractional derivative from competing mechanisms such as delayed feedback, unobserved environmental variables, colored process noise, or time-varying parameters.

This paper proposes to move beyond passive model fitting and ask a more operational question:

> **Which perturbations, measurement schedules, and observed variables make ecological memory empirically identifiable?**

The study will formulate the problem as one of optimal experimental design. Candidate interventions—such as prey removal, predator release, nutrient pulses, temporary refuges, or temperature shifts—will be optimized to maximize discrimination among:

1. classical integer-order dynamics;
2. Caputo fractional dynamics;
3. distributed-order fractional dynamics;
4. delay differential equations;
5. finite-dimensional latent-state models;
6. nonparametric memory-kernel models.

The main output will be a reproducible framework that selects experimental inputs and observation schedules capable of distinguishing memory mechanisms with minimum cost and ecological disturbance.

---

## 2. Scientific motivation

A fitted fractional order \(\alpha<1\) is not sufficient evidence of genuine biological memory. Similar trajectories can be generated by

\[
\text{fractional memory}
\approx
\text{distributed delay}
\approx
\text{latent environmental state}
\approx
\text{colored stochastic forcing}
\approx
\text{parameter drift}.
\]

Passive ecological records are especially vulnerable to this ambiguity because they often have:

- limited sample length;
- irregular observation times;
- low temporal resolution;
- incomplete state observation;
- substantial measurement error;
- uncontrolled environmental forcing;
- no deliberate excitation of the system.

System identification theory shows that parameters and model structures become observable only when the system is sufficiently excited. Ecological studies rarely optimize perturbations specifically to discriminate long-memory mechanisms.

The proposed paper addresses this gap by designing interventions that amplify differences between candidate models while respecting ethical, logistical, and biological constraints.

## 3. Central research question

> **What experimental intervention and measurement strategy maximizes the ability to distinguish fractional memory from delayed, latent-state, and classical ecological dynamics?**

### RQ1 — Input design

Which perturbation waveform is most informative?

Candidate forms include:

- single pulse;
- repeated pulses;
- step intervention;
- chirp or multiscale excitation;
- pseudorandom binary sequence;
- periodic forcing;
- state-dependent intervention.

### RQ2 — Observation design

Which variables should be observed, and at what times?

Possible choices include:

- prey only;
- predator only;
- both populations;
- environmental covariates;
- reproduction or mortality proxies;
- high-frequency sampling around interventions;
- adaptive observation times.

### RQ3 — Model discrimination

Which design best separates

\[
M_{\mathrm{ODE}},
\quad
M_{\mathrm{Caputo}},
\quad
M_{\mathrm{delay}},
\quad
M_{\mathrm{latent}},
\quad
M_{\mathrm{kernel}}?
\]

### RQ4 — Parameter identifiability

Does the design improve recovery of:

- fractional order \(\alpha\);
- delay \(\tau\);
- kernel-decay parameters;
- ecological interaction parameters;
- Allee thresholds;
- environmental-state time constants?

### RQ5 — Robustness

Does the selected design remain informative under:

- misspecified priors;
- observation noise;
- process noise;
- uncertain initial conditions;
- partial observability;
- model discrepancy?

## 4. Core hypotheses

### H1

**Multiscale and persistently exciting perturbations will discriminate ecological memory mechanisms substantially better than passive observation, single shocks, or purely periodic forcing.**

### H2

**The most informative design will depend strongly on whether both ecological state variables and at least one environmental covariate are observed.**

### H3

**Designs optimized for parameter estimation will not necessarily be optimal for model discrimination.**

For example, a design that estimates \(\alpha\) precisely under a Caputo model may still fail to distinguish that model from a latent-state approximation.

## 5. Candidate dynamical models

Let \(x(t)\) denote prey abundance, \(y(t)\) predator abundance, and \(u(t)\) an externally controlled intervention.

### Model M0 — Classical predator–prey ODE

\[
\dot{x}
=
f_x(x,y;\theta)+B_xu(t),
\]

\[
\dot{y}
=
f_y(x,y;\theta)+B_yu(t).
\]

A concrete baseline may use logistic prey growth and a Holling type II response:

\[
f_x
=
rx\left(1-\frac{x}{K}\right)
-
\frac{axy}{1+ahx},
\]

\[
f_y
=
e\frac{axy}{1+ahx}
-
my.
\]

### Model M1 — Caputo fractional model

\[
{}^{C}D_t^\alpha x
=
f_x(x,y;\theta)+B_xu(t),
\]

\[
{}^{C}D_t^\beta y
=
f_y(x,y;\theta)+B_yu(t).
\]

Possible restrictions include:

- common order \(\alpha=\beta\);
- component-specific orders;
- fixed initial-history assumptions;
- biologically constrained priors.

### Model M2 — Distributed-order model

\[
\int_0^1 w_x(q)\,{}^{C}D_t^q x\,dq
=
f_x(x,y;\theta)+B_xu(t),
\]

\[
\int_0^1 w_y(q)\,{}^{C}D_t^q y\,dq
=
f_y(x,y;\theta)+B_yu(t).
\]

### Model M3 — Delay differential model

\[
\dot{x}(t)
=
f_x\bigl(x(t),y(t),x(t-\tau_x),y(t-\tau_y);\theta\bigr)
+
B_xu(t),
\]

\[
\dot{y}(t)
=
f_y\bigl(x(t),y(t),x(t-\tau_x),y(t-\tau_y);\theta\bigr)
+
B_yu(t).
\]

### Model M4 — Latent environmental-state model

\[
\dot{x}
=
f_x(x,y,z;\theta)+B_xu(t),
\]

\[
\dot{y}
=
f_y(x,y,z;\theta)+B_yu(t),
\]

\[
\dot{z}
=
-\lambda z + Gu(t)+\eta(t).
\]

### Model M5 — Learned memory-kernel model

\[
\dot{x}(t)
=
f_x(x(t),y(t);\theta)
+
\int_0^t K_x(t-s;\phi_x)g_x(x(s),y(s))\,ds
+
B_xu(t),
\]

\[
\dot{y}(t)
=
f_y(x(t),y(t);\theta)
+
\int_0^t K_y(t-s;\phi_y)g_y(x(s),y(s))\,ds
+
B_yu(t).
\]

The kernels may be represented by:

- positive exponential mixtures;
- B-spline bases;
- sparse kernel dictionaries;
- Gaussian-process priors;
- constrained neural operators.

## 6. Experimental interventions

Define a design as

\[
d=(u(t),\mathcal{T},\mathcal{O}),
\]

where \(u(t)\) is the intervention, \(\mathcal{T}=\{t_1,\dots,t_n\}\) the measurement schedule, and \(\mathcal{O}\) the observed variables.

### A. Single pulse

\[
u(t)=A\,\mathbf{1}_{[t_0,t_0+\Delta]}(t).
\]

This is simple but may not separate long-memory from a slowly decaying latent state.

### B. Repeated pulses

\[
u(t)=\sum_{k=1}^m A_k
\mathbf{1}_{[t_k,t_k+\Delta_k]}(t).
\]

The spacing can be optimized to probe memory decay.

### C. Multiscale pulse train

Use several timescales:

\[
\Delta t_1 < \Delta t_2 < \cdots < \Delta t_m.
\]

This may be especially informative for power-law kernels.

### D. Chirp forcing

\[
u(t)=A\sin\left(
2\pi\left(f_0t+\frac{1}{2}kt^2\right)
\right).
\]

A frequency sweep can expose differences in phase lag and amplitude response.

### E. Pseudorandom binary sequence

\[
u(t)\in\{-A,+A\}
\]

with controlled switching. This is useful for persistent excitation.

### F. Adaptive intervention

Choose the next perturbation using the current posterior:

\[
d_{n+1}
=
\arg\max_d
\mathbb{E}
\left[
U(d,Y_{n+1})
\mid
Y_{1:n}
\right].
\]

## 7. Design objectives

### 7.1 Expected information gain

For model \(M\), parameters \(\vartheta\), future data \(Y\), and design \(d\):

\[
U_{\mathrm{EIG}}(d)
=
\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid d}
\left[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
\left(
p(\vartheta,M\mid Y,d)
\Vert
p(\vartheta,M)
\right)
\right].
\]

### 7.2 Model-discrimination utility

\[
U_{\mathrm{MD}}(d)
=
I(M;Y\mid d),
\]

where \(I\) is mutual information.

### 7.3 Pairwise predictive divergence

\[
U_{ij}(d)
=
\mathbb{E}
\left[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
\left(
p(Y\mid M_i,d)
\Vert
p(Y\mid M_j,d)
\right)
\right].
\]

A robust design may maximize the minimum pairwise divergence:

\[
d^\ast
=
\arg\max_d
\min_{i\neq j}
U_{ij}(d).
\]

### 7.4 Fisher-information criterion

\[
d^\ast
=
\arg\max_d
\log\det I(\vartheta;d).
\]

This is computationally cheaper but may be unreliable under nonlinear, multimodal, or structurally ambiguous models.

### 7.5 Decision-focused utility

The design may target a management-relevant property such as extinction risk or proximity to a bifurcation:

\[
U_{\mathrm{decision}}(d)
=
-\mathbb{E}
\left[
L\bigl(R,\widehat{R}(Y,d)\bigr)
\right].
\]

## 8. Constraints

### Intervention constraints

\[
|u(t)|\leq u_{\max},
\]

\[
\int_0^T |u(t)|\,dt\leq B.
\]

### Safety constraints

\[
x(t)\geq x_{\min},
\qquad
y(t)\geq y_{\min}
\]

with high probability or for all admissible parameters.

### Sampling constraints

- fixed number of samples;
- minimum interval between observations;
- laboratory operating windows;
- cost per measured variable;
- destructive versus non-destructive sampling.

The overall problem is

\[
\max_d U(d)
\]

subject to

\[
C_j(d)\leq 0,
\qquad j=1,\dots,J.
\]

## 9. Proposed methodology

### Phase 1 — Structural pre-analysis

1. Derive local observability conditions.
2. Determine whether \(\alpha\) and ecological parameters are confounded.
3. Identify which variables must be observed.
4. Characterize input classes that cannot distinguish candidate models.
5. Compare frequency responses near equilibrium.

### Phase 2 — Simulation benchmark

For every candidate design:

1. simulate future observations;
2. fit all candidate models;
3. compute posterior model probabilities;
4. evaluate parameter recovery;
5. measure predictive performance;
6. quantify decision errors.

### Phase 3 — Optimization

Candidate algorithms:

- gradient-based design using differentiable solvers;
- stochastic-gradient estimators for information gain;
- Bayesian optimization;
- sequential Monte Carlo design;
- dynamic programming;
- reinforcement learning for closed-loop interventions.

### Phase 4 — Robustness analysis

Repeat under:

- alternative priors;
- parameter uncertainty;
- model discrepancy;
- non-Gaussian observation noise;
- missing observations;
- uncertain intervention amplitude;
- solver approximation error.

### Phase 5 — Empirical or laboratory demonstration

Possible systems:

- microbial predator–prey experiments;
- plankton microcosms;
- laboratory insect systems;
- a calibrated ecological digital twin.

## 11. Potential theoretical results

### Theorem A — Non-discriminating input class

Prove that a specified family of inputs cannot distinguish a fractional linearization from a finite-dimensional latent-state approximation over a finite observation horizon.

### Theorem B — Persistent-excitation condition

Provide sufficient conditions under which an input \(u(t)\) yields local identifiability of \((\theta,\alpha)\).

### Theorem C — Frequency-separation result

Show that a suitable frequency set separates power-law from exponential-memory responses.

### Theorem D — Sample-complexity lower bound

Derive a bound on the observation horizon or number of samples required to distinguish two memory mechanisms at a prescribed error probability.

### Theorem E — Safe informative design

Prove the existence of an intervention satisfying both

\[
U(d)\geq U_{\min}
\]

and

\[
x(t),y(t)\in\mathcal{V}
\]

for all admissible parameters.

## 14. Risks and mitigation

### Risk 1 — The optimal design is model-dependent

**Mitigation:** use robust maximin or Bayesian model-averaged design.

### Risk 2 — Information-gain estimation is computationally expensive

**Mitigation:** use amortized posterior estimators, variational bounds, surrogate models, multi-fidelity solvers, and exponential-sum approximations.

### Risk 3 — The intervention is biologically unrealistic

**Mitigation:** optimize only over implementable intervention families and impose operational constraints from the outset.

### Risk 4 — Physical validation is unavailable

**Mitigation:** publish a rigorous computational-design paper using calibrated digital twins, followed by laboratory validation.

### Risk 5 — Model discrepancy dominates memory effects

**Mitigation:** include discrepancy terms, latent variables, and out-of-family stress tests.

## 19. One-paragraph pitch

This paper develops an optimal experimental-design framework for determining whether ecological population dynamics exhibit genuine fractional memory. Rather than fitting competing models to passively observed time series, it selects perturbations, observed variables, and sampling times that maximize discrimination among classical, fractional, delayed, latent-state, and learned-kernel systems. Using Bayesian information gain, robust optimization, identifiability analysis, and safety-constrained interventions, the study identifies which ecological experiments can reveal the shape and timescale of memory without endangering the system. The outcome will be both a theoretical contribution to non-Markovian system identification and a practical protocol for testing fractional ecological models experimentally.
