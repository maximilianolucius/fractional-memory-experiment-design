\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Fractional derivatives are widely used to represent hereditary and nonlocal effects in dynamical systems~\cite{F01,F02,F03,F04}, including predator--prey models~\cite{E06,E07,E08,E09}. The central empirical difficulty is that a fitted fractional order does not by itself identify a fractional mechanism: over finite horizons, delayed feedback and finite-dimensional latent states can reproduce similar trajectories. Experiment design for fractional-order identification already exists~\cite{FOED01,FOED02,FOED03,FOED04}, while safety-constrained information acquisition and safe active model discrimination are established general problems~\cite{SAFE26A,SAFE26B}. We therefore focus on a narrower question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Which perturbations and measurements can distinguish fractional memory from delayed or latent alternatives in a strong-Allee ecological system, and when does ecological safety make that discrimination fundamentally difficult?}
\end{quote}

The ecological backbone is deliberately inherited from our previous validated study~\cite{E03,E04,E05,P01}, so that the present work can isolate a different scientific question: \emph{experimental discrimination of memory mechanisms}. The previous paper established the equilibrium/stability structure, the Matignon boundary, extinction and boundedness certificates, and the failure of a naive Euler surrogate. None of those results is claimed as a contribution here. In the present paper they serve only to define the operating regime and the ecological safety geometry. The new objects are the competing ODE/Caputo/delay/latent response laws, controlled perturbations and observation channels, finite-horizon discrimination bounds, the combined fractional-delay stress test, and the numerical experiment-design benchmark. The ODE and Caputo mechanisms share the inherited ecological equilibrium and Jacobian; retarded-delay and finite-state latent models are calibrated rival response laws compared through matched inputs, observations, and physical units.

\paragraph{Explicit relationship to the previously submitted paper.}
Because the two manuscripts belong to the same research sequence, the reuse boundary is stated explicitly rather than left implicit. The present manuscript uses the \emph{same ecological backbone}: the strong-Allee predator--prey equations, the locked ecological parameters, the coexistence equilibrium, and the corresponding Jacobian are inherited from the previously submitted companion paper~\cite{P01}. Those ingredients are not reintroduced as new results and are not part of the novelty claim. They provide a controlled test bed on which a different problem is posed: how to design perturbations and observations that discriminate between alternative memory laws. This separation is also important methodologically. A stability certificate answers whether a specified Caputo model is locally or globally consistent with a dynamical regime; it does not answer whether the Caputo mechanism can be distinguished from an observationally similar delay or latent-state mechanism. Conversely, the present discrimination results do not recertify the ecological backbone. They condition on the previously established operating geometry and ask what additional perturbation/measurement information is required to tell the candidate memory laws apart.

\paragraph{Main contributions.}
The paper is organized around four contributions, rather than around a long sequence of standalone theorems.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Structural and finite-horizon separation.} We show that the collocated Caputo transfer has a noninteger high-frequency signature that cannot be exactly reproduced by finite rational latent models or by the declared retarded-delay class. At the same time, finite exponential mixtures approximate the fractional kernel on a finite horizon, creating a complexity-dependent discrimination barrier.
    \item \textbf{Certified prey-response testing obstruction.} Directly on the linearized ecological prey channel, a pole/branch decomposition yields interval-enclosed finite-state approximations. Under a declared pulse budget and Gaussian observations, the corresponding minimax testing lower bound approaches chance as latent complexity grows.
    \item \textbf{Numerical validation and a new fractional-delay stress test.} In addition to the calibrated ODE, Caputo, DDE, and latent benchmark, we introduce a combined fractional-delay model. It continuously connects the pure Caputo limit ($\tau=0$) to the DDE limit ($\alpha=1$), allowing direct simulation of how fractional order and delay jointly alter phase, transient amplitude, safety margin, and experimental discriminability.
    \item \textbf{Safety--informativeness trade-off.} Across six input families, broadband excitation improves memory discrimination but increases the rate of crossing the strong-Allee threshold. Transient inputs are safer numerically but less informative, making the constrained experiment-design problem visible rather than merely theoretical.
\end{enumerate}

The detailed proofs, secondary analytical results, Bayesian sequential extension, and solver derivations are moved to the Supplementary Material. The main paper retains only the analytical statements required to interpret the numerical results.
