\subsection{From exact signatures to finite-horizon ambiguity}
The two frequency-domain panels should be read together. The noninteger high-frequency decay and phase give a structural signature of Caputo dynamics, but an experiment does not observe an asymptotic half-line of frequencies. It observes a finite bandwidth over a finite time window and with finite noise. A sufficiently flexible latent model can therefore mimic the relevant part of the response without being analytically identical to the fractional transfer. This is why the structural theorem and the finite-horizon impossibility theorem are complementary rather than competing statements.

The ecological prey-response calculation makes the same point directly in the controlled population channel. For $\alpha=0.85$ and $T=12$, Table~\ref{tab:state-approx} reports certified $L^1$ response bounds together with the corresponding Pinsker lower bounds under the declared pulse and $\sigma=0.10$. The approximation improves by almost two orders of magnitude between $m=4$ and $m=32$, and the lower bound correspondingly approaches the equal-prior chance level $1/2$.

The table is deliberately a one-sided statement. A large lower bound on $P_e^*$ proves that the specified experiment cannot reliably distinguish the focal fractional response from at least one rival in the admitted finite-state class; the converse is not claimed. Cells in which the bound is small are therefore \emph{inconclusive}, not automatically discriminable. This interpretation is preserved in the atlas of Figure~\ref{fig:atlas}.

The finite-horizon approximation results then explain why frequency-domain separation does not automatically translate into easy model selection. The safe memory-discrimination atlas in Figure~\ref{fig:atlas} displays the protocol-restricted lower bound on testing error across fractional order, Allee parameter, and latent complexity. Cells outside the focal Matignon-stable regime are explicitly excluded from the headline interpretation.
