\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The restructured results support three conclusions.

First, \emph{structural identifiability and practical discrimination are different questions}. The Caputo transfer has an exact noninteger signature, yet finite latent models approximate the same response increasingly well on finite horizons. The testing bounds quantify this gap and explain why a flexible latent alternative can remain difficult even when the underlying model classes are analytically distinct.

Second, \emph{delay and fractional memory should not be treated as mutually exclusive numerical explanations}. The combined fractional-delay simulations show a continuous bridge between the pure Caputo and pure DDE limits. Delay systematically shifts the predator response, while the fractional order controls the broader relaxation structure. The interior of the $(\alpha,\tau)$ plane contains dynamics that can be poorly represented by either pure limit, which is precisely why direct numerical stress testing is valuable.

Third, \emph{the most informative perturbation is not automatically the safest}. The same conclusion appears in the original four-class benchmark and in the new fractional-delay experiments. Broadband inputs enlarge response differences but more often drive prey below the strong-Allee threshold. This tension is ecological, not merely numerical: crossing $A$ changes the long-term basin structure and invalidates an experiment designed only around local identifiability.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The certified ecological response theorem is linearized and prey-channel specific; a full-state hierarchy-wide safety closure remains open. The fractional-delay study is numerical and uses a constant equilibrium history, one delayed trophic pathway, and a targeted parameter grid. The benchmark uses BIC rather than fully Bayesian evidence and does not execute adaptive design. These restrictions are stated explicitly to separate proved results from computational evidence.

\paragraph{Relation to the previously submitted companion paper.}
The present manuscript shares the strong-Allee ecological backbone and locked baseline parameterization with Ref.~\cite{P01}, but it does not repeat that paper's research question or headline results. Ref.~\cite{P01} concerns computer-assisted certification of Caputo equilibria, the Matignon stability boundary, extinction/boundedness, and the diagnostic failure of a forward-Euler surrogate. By contrast, the present work studies active discrimination among competing memory mechanisms. The structural transfer separation, finite-horizon latent approximation hierarchy, testing obstruction, certified prey-response approximation, six-family input-design study, four-class discrimination benchmark, and combined fractional-delay simulations are not results of Ref.~\cite{P01}. The inherited stability facts are used only to select admissible operating regimes and are explicitly attributed where they enter.



\paragraph{What is genuinely added by the present paper.}
The reuse of the same ecological backbone is a design choice, not a duplication of the previous study. Holding the ecology fixed makes it possible to attribute differences in the simulated observations to the memory law and to the experiment rather than to a changed population model. The new paper therefore starts where the companion paper stops. Once an operating point and stability region have been certified, one must still decide whether observed transients reveal a fractional mechanism uniquely, whether a delayed or finite latent model can mimic them, and which perturbations can separate those alternatives without leaving the safe ecological regime. These are distinct inferential questions, and they require different mathematics and different computations.

The contrast also explains why the figures are different. The companion paper's central visual object is a certified $(A,\alpha)$ stability atlas. In the present manuscript, the central visuals concern response separation, approximation error, waveform design, BIC confusion, fractional-delay trajectories, and the safety--informativeness frontier. The common model schematic is used only to anchor the reader in the shared ecology; none of the companion paper's certificate atlas or certificate-count results is reused as evidence for the new discrimination claims.

\paragraph{Relation to broader prior work.}
General safe information limits and safe active model discrimination are already established~\cite{SAFE26A,SAFE26B}; the contribution here is the interaction of finite-horizon fractional approximation, bounded latent complexity, strong-Allee ecological geometry, and direct numerical stress tests of the combined fractional-delay mechanism. The paper therefore does not claim novelty for safe experimental design in the abstract.


\paragraph{Implications for experimental identification.}
The experiments suggest that model selection should be treated as a controlled intervention problem rather than a passive curve-fitting exercise. With weak excitation, the four candidate mechanisms can produce trajectories that are close enough that BIC often defaults to the simpler ODE explanation. Increasing excitation improves separation but can move the system toward or across the Allee threshold. The combined fractional-delay study adds a second warning: even if a pure Caputo model and a pure DDE are individually distinguishable, an interior model containing both effects may occupy a response region that neither pure limit represents well. Consequently, experimental design should test composite alternatives rather than only the two endpoints one at a time.

A useful practical objective is therefore not ``maximize information'' in isolation, but maximize information subject to a trajectory-level safety margin and a declared rival-model complexity budget. The latter is essential because an unrestricted finite latent hierarchy can approximate fractional memory arbitrarily well on a fixed finite horizon. Reporting the allowed latent dimension, delay range, forcing bandwidth, and observation horizon is thus part of the scientific specification of the discrimination experiment, not a secondary implementation detail.
