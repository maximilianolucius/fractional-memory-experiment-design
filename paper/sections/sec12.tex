\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

This section interprets the reproducible benchmark results presented in Section~\\ref{sec:benchmark} and situates them within the theoretical framework developed in Sections~\\ref{sec:separation}--\\ref{sec:bayesian}.  The benchmark demonstrates that the optimal input--output design derived from the structural separation theorem (T4--T7) and the finite-horizon impossibility barrier (T8--T10) successfully discriminates between fractional (Caputo), delayed (DDE) and latent (finite-dimensional) memory models in the strong-Allee predator--prey benchmark of Source Pack (see \\texttt{source\_pack/04\_EXACT\_STRONG\_ALLEE\_BASELINE.md}).  The posterior odds, KL divergences and safety-violation rates reported in Section~\\ref{sec:benchmark} confirm that the optimal experiment designed via the Bayesian safety-constrained criterion (T16--T17) yields maximal model discriminability while respecting the safety rectangle derived from the Allee threshold.

The discrimination power stems from the frequency-domain separation of the memory kernels: fractional memory exhibits a power-law frequency response, delayed memory introduces a pure delay term, and latent memory yields a rational approximation with finite-dimensional poles.  The optimal input spectrum, shaped by the Fisher information matrix derived in Sections~\ref{sec:design} and~\ref{sec:bayesian}, concentrates energy at frequencies where these kernels differ most, thereby amplifying the KL divergence between model posteriors.  The safety-constrained design further ensures that the excitation does not push the system below the Allee threshold, a critical requirement for ecological experiments where extinction must be avoided.

Despite its empirical success, the benchmark has several limitations that should be acknowledged when interpreting the results and planning future work.  First, the simulation assumes deterministic dynamics corrupted only by additive Gaussian measurement noise.  Real ecological data are subject to demographic and environmental stochasticity, which may obscure the spectral signatures exploited here.  Second, the finite-horizon impossibility result (T10) implies that discrimination becomes increasingly difficult as the experiment duration approaches the memory horizon; our benchmark uses a horizon safely below this limit, but longer-term studies may require alternative designs such as periodic renewal or reset protocols.  Third, the strong Allee assumption imposes a hard safety constraint; for systems with soft thresholds or Allee effects that depend on higher-order moments, the rectangle-based safety condition of Section~\ref{sec:safety} may be either overly conservative or insufficient.

Compared with existing literature on optimal experimental design (OED) for dynamical systems~\cite{O01,O05} and fractional-order system identification~\cite{I02,F02}, our approach contributes three novel elements.  First, we explicitly incorporate a hard safety constraint derived from an ecological Allee effect, transforming the OED problem into a constrained information-maximization task.  Second, we exploit the structural separation of memory kernels in the frequency domain to construct inputs that are provably discriminative for infinite-dimensional memory models, a direction less explored in the OED literature which often focuses on parametric models.  Third, we provide a nested Monte-Carlo estimator with a four-tier variance-reduction hierarchy that remains computationally tractable for nonlinear Bayesian updating while retaining theoretical guarantees of consistency under the calibration contract of Section~\ref{sec:bayesian}.

The implications for experimental practice in ecological and biological systems with memory are significant.  Practitioners should first attempt to identify the dominant memory kernel type through low-amplitude spectral excitation (e.g., chirp or multisine signals) designed according to the sensitivity functions of Sections~\ref{sec:design} and~\ref{sec:bayesian}.  If safety constraints are known (e.g., minimum viable population), the safety-constrained OED algorithm of Section~\ref{sec:safety} can be employed to guarantee that the excitation remains within viable bounds.  Finally, the Bayesian adaptive scheme of Section~\ref{sec:bayesian} allows sequential refinement of the experiment as data accumulate, reducing the need for extensive pilot studies.

Future work may extend this framework along several axes.  (i) Stochastic dynamics: incorporating demographic and environmental noise into the Fisher information matrix and designing robust inputs that maximize worst-case discriminability.  (ii) Heterogeneous memory: extending the latent-memory model to distributed-order fractional derivatives or multiple delay scales.  (iii) Closed-loop implementation: real-time estimation and adaptive input generation on embedded hardware for in-situ ecological experimentation.  (iv) Cross-validation with alternative model classes such as Michaelis--Menten kinetics with delay or distributed-state representations of memory.  (v) Application to other ecological motifs (e.g., mutualism, competition) where memory effects arise from delayed density dependence or legacy effects.

In summary, the benchmark validates the theoretical pipeline from structural separation and finite-horizon limitations to safe, optimal input design and Bayesian adaptive sequencing.  The approach provides a principled, safety-aware methodology for discriminating memory mechanisms in ecological systems, bridging the gap between abstract memory theory and actionable experimental protocols.
